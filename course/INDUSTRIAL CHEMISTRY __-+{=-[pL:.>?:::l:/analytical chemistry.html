Analytical Chemistry

Analytical chemistry is the science of measuring what is in a sample and how much of it is there. It splits into qualitative (what) and quantitative (how much).

Definition

Study of separation, identification, and quantification of chemical components in materials. Often a separation step precedes measurement.

Two main branches

  • Qualitative analysis — identifies species present (ions, functional groups).
  • Quantitative analysis — measures amounts or concentrations.

Classical methods

Also called traditional methods. They rely on chemical reactions and measuring mass or volume after exhaustive reaction.

  • Gravimetric analysis — isolate and weigh a product to find amount of analyte.
  • Volumetric analysis — titrations where volume of titrant gives concentration.

Instrumental methods

Modern techniques measure a physical signal related to concentration. Faster and often more sensitive than classical methods.

  • Spectroscopy — measures light absorption or emission (UV‑Vis, IR).
  • Chromatography — separates components (GC, HPLC) then detects them.
  • Electrochemical — measures current or potential (potentiometry, voltammetry).

How quantification works

Most instrumental methods use a calibration curve: measure known standards, plot signal vs concentration, then use the curve to find unknowns.

Example: Prepare standard solutions of known concentration, measure absorbance in a UV‑Vis spectrophotometer, plot absorbance vs concentration, then read concentration of unknown from its absorbance.

Qualitative tools and tricks

  • Flame tests — simple color test for metal ions (Na gives yellow, Cu gives green/blue).
  • Spot tests — small chemical reactions on paper or a spot plate to indicate presence of a group.
  • Chromatograms — separate mixture components visually or with detectors.

Careers and applications

Analytical chemists work in industry, environmental monitoring, forensics, pharmaceuticals, food testing, and research labs.

Tip: Learn both the theory of instruments and practical sample preparation. Good results depend more on sample handling than on the instrument itself.

Organic Chemistry Notes

Focused, student-friendly explanations of bonding, hybridization, delocalization, inductive effect, hyperconjugation, and charge transfer.

Structure and Bonding

Atoms form bonds by overlapping orbitals. Two common bond types:

  • Sigma bonds σ — head‑on overlap; single bonds are σ and are strong and localized between two atoms.
  • Pi bonds π — side‑by‑side overlap of p orbitals; found in double and triple bonds and can be delocalized across several atoms.
Example: Ethane C–C is a σ bond; ethene C=C has one σ and one π bond; acetylene C≡C has one σ and two π bonds.

Hybridization

Hybridization mixes atomic orbitals to explain molecular geometry and bond angles.

  • sp³ — tetrahedral, 109.5°, four σ bonds (example CH₄).
  • sp² — trigonal planar, 120°, one π available (example C in C=C).
  • sp — linear, 180°, two π available (example C in C≡C).
Example: In ethene each carbon is sp²; the unhybridized p orbitals form the π bond that makes the double bond reactive to additions.

Localized versus Delocalized Bonds

Localized electrons are confined to a bond or lone pair. Delocalized electrons are spread over several atoms, stabilizing the molecule through resonance.

  • Localized — single bond or lone pair between two atoms.
  • Delocalized — π electrons in conjugated systems like benzene are shared across the ring.
Example: Benzene has six π electrons delocalized over six carbons, giving aromatic stability and unique reactivity.

Inductive Effect

Inductive effect is electron withdrawal or donation transmitted through σ bonds due to electronegativity differences. It weakens with distance.

  • –I groups withdraw electron density (example NO₂), stabilizing adjacent positive charges.
  • +I groups donate electron density (alkyl groups), destabilizing adjacent positive charges.
Example: A nitro group on a benzene ring withdraws electron density and makes electrophilic substitution harder at nearby positions.

Hyperconjugation

Hyperconjugation is stabilization by overlap of σ C–H or C–C bonds with an adjacent empty or partially filled p or π orbital. It explains carbocation stability.

  • More adjacent C–H bonds → more hyperconjugation → greater stabilization.
  • Order of carbocation stability: tertiary > secondary > primary > methyl.
Example: A tertiary carbocation is stabilized by nine hyperconjugative C–H interactions compared to three in a primary carbocation.

Charge Transfer

Charge transfer complexes form when electrons partially move from a donor to an acceptor. These interactions can change color and conductivity.

Example: A donor molecule plus an acceptor molecule can form a colored complex because of a new absorption band from charge transfer.

Examples Summary and Quick Study Tips

Quick Examples

  • UV‑Vis calibration — make standards, measure absorbance, plot calibration curve, read unknown concentration.
  • Flame test — Na gives yellow flame; K gives lilac; Cu gives green/blue.
  • Benzene — delocalized π electrons; aromatic stability; resists addition reactions that break aromaticity.
  • Carbocation stability — tertiary stabilized by inductive effect and hyperconjugation; used to predict reaction pathways.

Study Tips

  • For analytical chemistry, practice sample preparation and simple calibration curves — these skills give reliable results fast.
  • For organic chemistry, draw structures, label hybridization, and practice resonance forms to see delocalization.
  • Use small experiments or simulations to connect theory to observation: e.g., run a simple titration or view a flame test safely.
