Organic Chemistry (CH260)

Organic Chemistry Notes (CH260)

1. Classification

Organic compounds are carbon-based. They are divided into:

  • Hydrocarbons: Alkanes, alkenes, alkynes, aromatics.
  • Functionalized compounds: Alcohols, halides, acids, esters, amines.
  • Natural sources: Plant oils, animal fats, fossil fuels.
  • Importance: Basis of life (DNA, proteins, carbohydrates), fuels, plastics, medicines.

2. Structure and Bonding

Carbon forms covalent bonds. Hybridization explains geometry:

  • sp: Linear, 180°. Example: acetylene (C≡C).
  • sp2: Trigonal planar, 120°. Example: ethylene (C=C).
  • sp3: Tetrahedral, 109.5°. Example: methane (CH4).

Hybridization determines shape, bond angles, and reactivity.

3. Mechanism of Organic Reactions

Organic reactions are explained by electron movement. Curved arrows show how electrons shift:

  • Nucleophilic attack: Electron-rich species attacks electron-deficient center.
  • Electrophilic addition: Double bond reacts with electrophile (e.g., HBr + alkene).
  • Substitution: SN1 (carbocation intermediate), SN2 (backside attack, inversion).

4. Types of Reagents

  • Electrophiles: Electron-deficient, accept electrons. Examples: H+, NO2+, carbocations.
  • Nucleophiles: Electron-rich, donate electrons. Examples: OH–, NH3, halide ions.

5. Stereochemistry I

Isomerism = same formula, different arrangement.

  • Structural isomers: Different connectivity.
  • Stereoisomers: Same connectivity, different spatial arrangement.
  • Optical isomers: Mirror images (enantiomers). Important in drug design.

6. Stereochemistry II

E/Z system describes double bond geometry:

  • E (entgegen): Higher priority groups on opposite sides.
  • Z (zusammen): Higher priority groups on same side.

7. Alkanes and Cycloalkanes

Alkanes: saturated hydrocarbons (single bonds). Properties: relatively unreactive, used as fuels.

Cycloalkanes: ring structures. Stability depends on ring size (cyclopropane strained, cyclohexane stable).

8. Conformational Analysis

Cyclohexane adopts chair, boat, twist conformations. Chair is most stable. Substituents prefer equatorial positions to reduce steric hindrance.

9. Arenes and Aromaticity

Benzene is the classic aromatic compound. Aromaticity requires:

  • Cyclic, planar structure.
  • Conjugated π system.
  • 4n+2 π electrons (Hückel’s rule).

Aromatic compounds undergo substitution (not addition) to preserve stability.

10. Alkenes

Contain C=C double bonds. Reactions include:

  • Dehydration of alcohols → alkenes.
  • Dehydrohalogenation of alkyl halides.
  • Mechanisms: E1 (carbocation intermediate), E2 (concerted), E1cb (carbanion intermediate).

11. Cycloalkenes, Dienes, and Alkynes

Unsaturated hydrocarbons:

  • Cycloalkenes: cyclic double bonds.
  • Dienes: two double bonds (conjugated dienes are especially reactive, e.g., Diels–Alder reaction).
  • Alkynes: triple bonds, linear geometry, acidic hydrogen at terminal carbon.

12. Alkyl Halides

Alkyl halides: carbon bonded to halogen. Prepared by halogenation of alkanes. Reactivity depends on substitution (primary, secondary, tertiary).

13. Aryl Halides

Aryl halides: halogen attached to aromatic ring. Undergo substitution reactions (nuclear and side chain). Less reactive than alkyl halides due to resonance stabilization.

14. Alcohols

Alcohols: compounds with –OH group. Classified as primary, secondary, tertiary. Prepared by reduction of aldehydes, ketones, carboxylic acids, and esters. Important solvents and intermediates.
