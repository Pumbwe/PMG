Introduction to Industrial Chemistry

Industrial chemistry is the translation of laboratory chemistry into large-scale production of goods. Typical examples include:

  • Saponification scaled to soap manufacturing.
  • Fermentation scaled to beer brewing.
  • Polymerization scaled to production of resins and plastics.
  • Organic synthesis scaled to pharmaceutical manufacture.

Chemistry vs Industrial Chemistry

Chemistry

Chemistry is the branch of natural science that deals with chemical substances, their properties, the changes they undergo, and the laws describing these changes.

Industrial chemistry

Industrial chemistry uses chemical and physical processes to transform raw materials into products beneficial to society, focusing on process development, scale-up, and manufacturing.

Understanding Industrial Chemistry

Industrial chemistry focuses on understanding and developing processes in chemical plants. The general objective of a chemical plant is to create new material wealth via chemical or biological transformation and/or separation of materials. Chemical plants use specialized equipment, units, and technology in manufacturing.

Industrial Chemistry vs Chemical Engineering

Industrial chemistry studies processes used to produce compounds from raw materials. Chemical engineering focuses on the design of processes used to synthesize, convert, transform, or transport chemicals — including equipment design and process optimization.

Industrial Scale Equipment

  • Reactor tanks
  • Mixing tanks
  • Distillation towers
  • Evaporation chambers
  • Crushers, grinders, concentrators, mills
  • Furnaces

Scope of Industrial Chemistry

Industrial chemistry includes industries such as:

  • Petrochemicals (polymers for plastics and synthetic fibers)
  • Inorganic chemicals (acids and alkalis)
  • Agricultural chemicals (fertilizers, pesticides, herbicides)
  • Industrial gases, specialty chemicals, and pharmaceuticals

Chemical Industries and Products

Examples of chemical industries and products:

  • Cement and lime manufacturing
  • Paints, coatings and dyes
  • Breweries
  • Pharmaceuticals
  • Petroleum refineries
  • Soap and cosmetics manufacturing
  • Agrochemical manufacturing
  • Glass and plastic manufacturing

Product categories

  • Commodity chemicals — produced in large quantities (e.g., sulphuric acid, ammonia, sodium hydroxide)
  • Specialty chemicals — high value, low volume, performance-based (e.g., pigments, flavors, surfactants)
  • Fine chemicals — high-purity organic substances (e.g., APIs, peptides)

Raw Materials for the Chemical Industry

From the atmosphere

Air provides industrial gases such as N₂, O₂, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe. Atmospheric supply is effectively unlimited for many gases.

From the hydrosphere

Seawater is a source of sodium chloride, magnesium, and bromine.

From the lithosphere

Elements and minerals are obtained from the earth's crust as ores, coal, natural gas, and crude petroleum — both energy sources and chemical feedstocks.

From the biosphere

Vegetation and animals supply oils, fats, waxes, resins, sugar, natural fibres and leather for agro-based industries.

Chemical Processes and Units

Every industrial process is designed to produce a desired product from starting raw materials using energy through a succession of treatment steps. Treatments are either chemical (reactions) or physical (unit operations).

Typical process flow

Input materials → Process transformation → Output products

Feed materials are measured and fed into reactors or mixing tanks; transformations occur under controlled conditions to produce products for supply.

Material Balances

From the law of conservation of mass: mass can neither be created nor destroyed. Material balances determine amounts and compositions of streams in a process and form the basis for energy balances and economic evaluation.

Purpose

  • Determine amount and composition of each stream.
  • Provide data for energy balances and process evaluation.
  • Quantify environmental emissions and yields.

Assumptions

  • No transfer of mass to energy.
  • Mass is conserved for each element or compound on molar or weight basis.
  • Moles are conserved only when there is no reaction; volume is not conserved.

Balance categories

Processes without chemical reaction and processes with chemical reaction are treated differently. General relation: Raw materials = Products + Waste + Stored + Losses.

Worked Examples and Block Diagrams

Material balance calculations are often handled using block diagrams. Example block diagrams and simple mass balance sketches should be drawn for each process studied.
