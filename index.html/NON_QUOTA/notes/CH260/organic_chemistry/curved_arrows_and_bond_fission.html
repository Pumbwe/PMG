Curved Arrows: The Accounting System for Electron Movement

If you think of electrons as the currency of chemistry, reactions are transactions of electrons between atoms. Chemists have developed their own convention for showing transactions of electrons between atoms. This is the curved arrow formalism.

Understanding the location of electrons and being able to draw the curved arrows that depict the mechanisms by which the reactions occur is one of the most critical tools for learning organic chemistry since they allow you to understand what controls reactions, and how reactions proceed.

1. The Purpose of the Curved Arrow Formalism

The purpose of the curved arrow is to show the movement of electrons from one site to another.

Electrons move from the tail to the head. Most of the arrows that you will see have a double-barb at the head, representing the movement of a pair of electrons.

2. The Three Legal Moves

There are only three legal moves you can do with curved arrows. These three moves are for depiction of a pair of electrons moving from:

  • Lone pair → Bond
  • Bond → Lone pair
  • Bond → Bond

As with resonance, there are only three classes of reaction arrows.

3. Curved Arrows and Formal Charge Tracking

Curved arrows are also a way of tracking changes in formal charge:

  • Since a pair of electrons are being donated from the "tail", the atom at the tail will have a formal "loss" of one electron, making its charge more positive by 1.
  • Since a pair of electrons are being accepted at the "head", the atom at the head will have a formal "gain" of one electron, making its charge more negative by 1.

Example #1: Lone Pair → Bond

The first example shows a lone pair on a naked hydroxide ion going to hydronium ion (H+). The arrow shows the formation of a bond between O and H, with electrons from the lone pair on the oxygen.

Note the changes in formal charge: the "tail" becomes more positive, and the "head" becomes more negative.

  • "Tail": Oxygen is going from owning a pair of electrons to sharing it. So it "loses" an electron, resulting in it becoming more positive by 1.
  • "Head": Hydrogen is going from lacking to sharing. So, it "gains" an electron, becoming more negative by 1.

Example #2: Bond → Lone Pair

The second example shows the reverse reaction: dissociation of water to give H+ and hydroxide ion. Here, the arrow shows the breaking of the O-H bond, to end up as a lone pair on oxygen.

Note the changes in formal charge: at the "tail", hydrogen goes from "sharing" to "lacking". At the "head", oxygen goes from "sharing" to "owning".

  • "Tail": Hydrogen is going from sharing a pair of electrons to lacking it. So it "loses" an electron, resulting in it becoming more positive by 1.
  • "Head": Oxygen is going from sharing an electron pair to owning. So, it "gains" an electron, becoming more negative by 1.

Example #3: Bond → Bond

Finally, an example of a bond breaking to form another bond. Here, the arrow shows the breaking of a π bond between C1 and C2, and the formation of a new C–H bond between C1 and H.

  • "Tail": C2 is going from sharing a pair of electrons to lacking it. So it "loses" an electron, resulting in it becoming more positive by 1.
  • "Head": Hydrogen is going from lacking an electron pair to sharing. So, it "gains" an electron, becoming more negative by 1.

Curved Arrow Summary

  • Curved arrows flow from electron rich to electron poor.
  • Therefore, they start from lone pairs or bonds.
  • The charges in any step should always be balanced.
  • Remember to obey the rules of valence (e.g., Octet rule for C, N, O, F, etc.)
  • If electrons are taken out of a bond, then that bond is broken.
  • If electrons are placed between two atoms then it implies a bond is being made.

Bond Fission Mechanisms: Homolytic and Heterolytic Fission

Most chemical reactions involve the breakage of existing chemical bonds and the formation of new ones. However, chemical bonds can be broken in multiple ways. Furthermore, the manner in which a chemical bond breaks plays a vital role in deciding the overall outcome of the chemical reaction. The breakage of a chemical bond (usually a covalent bond) is often referred to as bond fission.

Homolytic Fission

Homolytic fission (sometimes referred to as hemolysis) is a type of bond fission that involves the dissociation of a given molecule wherein one electron is retained by each of the original fragments of the molecule.

Therefore, when a neutrally charged molecule is subjected to homolytic fission, two free radicals are obtained as the products (since each of the chemical species retains one electron from the bond pair).

The energy required to facilitate homolytic fission in a molecule is often referred to as the homolytic bond dissociation energy of the molecule.

When Does Homolytic Fission Occur?

Typically, a large amount of energy is required to spark the homolytic fission of a molecule. This is the reason why this type of bond fission only occurs in some cases:

  • When the molecule is subjected to ultraviolet radiation (electromagnetic radiation in the UV region).
  • When the molecule is subjected to the required amount of heat to overcome the required bond dissociation energy for homolytic fission.
  • When carbon compounds are subjected to extremely high temperatures in the absence of oxygen to facilitate the pyrolysis of the molecule.

Products of Homolytic Fission

AB → A• + B•

Result: Two free radicals (each with an unpaired electron)

Heterolytic Fission

Heterolytic fission, also known as heterolysis, is a type of bond fission in which a covalent bond between two chemical species is broken in an unequal manner, resulting in the bond pair of electrons being retained by one of the chemical species (while the other species does not retain any of the electrons from the bond pair).

When a neutrally charged molecule undergoes heterolytic fission, one of the products will have a positive charge (carbocation) whereas the other will have a negative charge (carbanion).

Products of Heterolytic Fission

AB → A+ + B- (or A- + B+)

Result: A cation (positively charged) and an anion (negatively charged)

Factors Influencing Heterolytic Fission

  • Electronegativity: The more electronegative atom tends to retain the electron pair.
  • Solvent polarity: Polar solvents favor heterolytic fission by stabilizing the ionic products.
  • Stability of carbocations: The stability of resulting carbocations (tertiary > secondary > primary) influences the likelihood and pathway.

Comparison: Homolytic vs. Heterolytic Fission

Feature Homolytic Fission Heterolytic Fission
Electron Distribution One electron to each fragment Both electrons to one fragment
Products Two free radicals (A•, B•) Cation and anion (A+, B-)
Energy Required High (homolytic BDE) Lower in polar solvents
Typical Conditions UV light, heat, pyrolysis Polar solvents, mild conditions
Charge Change Neutral products Charged products
Reaction Type Radical reactions Ionic/polar reactions

Key Takeaways

  • Curved arrows are essential for visualizing electron movement in organic reactions.
  • Three legal moves: lone pair → bond, bond → lone pair, bond → bond.
  • Formal charge tracking ensures electron accounting is correct.
  • Homolytic fission produces free radicals and requires significant energy.
  • Heterolytic fission produces ionic species and is favored in polar solvents.
  • Understanding bond fission mechanisms is crucial for predicting reaction pathways and products.
